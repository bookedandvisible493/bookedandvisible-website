Can you text or email customers to ask for a review in Canada?

By Viktor, founder of Booked & Visible · Published November 21, 2026

Asking customers for a review by text is quick, and it works. In Canada, though, texts and emails that promote a business fall under an anti-spam law called CASL. Here is what the law says, how it may apply to a review request, and a simple wording that covers the rules.

Quick answer

You can text or email customers to ask for a review. CASL doesn't name review requests, but a message with a commercial purpose needs consent, your business details and a working opt-out. A recent customer usually gives you implied consent, and treating every review request as covered is the safest approach.

Does CASL apply to a review request?

CASL covers a commercial electronic message: a text, email or similar message that, as one of its purposes, encourages someone to take part in a commercial activity. The law doesn't mention review requests by name.

A plain thank-you with a review link may not be commercial. A message that also promotes your services, an offer or a booking is more likely to be. You can't always tell, so the safest approach is to treat every review request as covered. See the CRTC's CASL questions and answers for the official guidance. This is general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, speak to a BC lawyer.

Do you need the customer's permission?

Yes, but a recent customer usually gives it. CASL recognizes two kinds of consent:

  • Express consent: the person clearly agreed to hear from you, in person, in writing or by ticking a box. It lasts until they withdraw it.
  • Implied consent: it applies in set cases and expires. An existing business relationship, such as a purchase or a contract, gives you up to 2 years. An inquiry gives you 6 months.

A customer whose job you finished last week almost always counts. A customer from three years ago doesn't, and you would need their express consent. Details are on the federal CASL consent page.

What must the message include?

Every commercial message needs three things:

  • Your business name.
  • Your mailing address and a phone number, email address or website. These must stay valid for at least 60 days after you send the message.
  • A working way to unsubscribe, at no cost, such as replying STOP to a text or a link in an email.

A text that covers all three might read: "Thanks for choosing Smith Plumbing today. A quick Google review helps a lot: [link]. Smith Plumbing, 123 Main St, Langley, BC. Reply STOP to opt out."

What happens when someone replies STOP?

You must stop messaging them as soon as you can, and no later than 10 business days after the request. It must cost them nothing. Keep a simple record: the date of the job, the date you sent the message, and the date of any opt-out.

CASL is separate from Google's rules for the review itself. Ask every customer the same way, never only the ones you expect to be happy, and never offer a discount or gift for a review. Can you get in trouble for asking customers for reviews? covers those rules.

Common questions

Do I need permission before texting a customer for a review?

Yes, but a recent customer usually covers it. An existing business relationship, such as a finished job, gives implied consent for up to 2 years.

What has to be in the text or email?

Your business name, your mailing address and a phone number, email or website that stays valid for 60 days, plus a free way to opt out, such as replying STOP.

What if a customer replies STOP?

Stop messaging them within 10 business days, at no cost to them. Note the date so you don't message them again by mistake.
